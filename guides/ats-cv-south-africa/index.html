What actually makes a CV pass or fail ATS screening

Not the buzzword. The specific, checkable rules a scanning system applies before a human ever reads your CV.

Most job applications in South Africa today pass through an Applicant Tracking System (ATS) before a person sees them. That system tries to pull structured data out of your CV: your name, dates, job titles, skills. If it can't extract a field cleanly, that information effectively doesn't exist to the recruiter, no matter how well it's written.

"ATS-optimised" gets used as a marketing label a lot. Here's what it actually means in practice, rule by rule.

Single column for anything that matters

A sidebar can hold a skills list or contact details. A job title, date, or employer name should never live only in a sidebar, because a scanning system can scatter or drop sidebar content entirely. The safest layout keeps your core work history in a straight single column, with a sidebar used only for supplementary information.

Standard section headings

"Work Experience," "Education," "Skills." Not "My Journey" or "What I Bring." A creative section title is a real parsing risk, not just a style choice, because the system is often matching against a list of expected headings to know what it's looking at.

Nothing hidden in a header or footer

Your phone number and email should sit in the main body of the document. Many scanning systems skip headers and footers entirely, so contact information placed there may as well not exist to the system reading it.

No icons standing in for words

A checkmark, a star rating, or an icon isn't read the same way as text by parsing software. Plain text and standard bullet points (solid circles, squares, or dashes) are the safe choice. Custom symbols, checkmarks, or arrow glyphs as bullet markers are not.

Real text, not a picture of text

A CV has to be built from selectable text in the PDF itself, never a scanned image or a PDF where the text is actually a picture. If you can't select and copy the words, a scanning system usually can't read them either.

Web-safe fonts only

A clean, standard font, one of the small set that's guaranteed to render the same way everywhere: Arial, Calibri, Cambria, Garamond, Georgia, Helvetica, Tahoma, Times New Roman, Verdana. No decorative or script typefaces, which can fail to embed correctly and break text extraction.

One page when one page is honest

Content should be sized to fit the page it belongs on, not padded with extra spacing or vague language to look fuller than it is. A shorter, honest CV reads better to both a scanning system and a person than a stretched one.

Why not just ask ChatGPT?

A general-purpose AI chat can write CV text. It doesn't know, by default, that a two-column layout can scatter your dates when a scanning system reads it, that a phone number placed in a header can go invisible to that same system, or that a creative section title can fail to register as work experience at all.
